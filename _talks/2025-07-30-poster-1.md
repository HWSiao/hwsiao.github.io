Abstract
======
Due to Taiwan's unique geographic location near the magnetic equator, man-made facilities and livelihood applications are particularly susceptible to ionospheric scintillation caused by Equatorial plasma bubbles (EPBs), which are features appeared in the ionospheric F region and characterized by significantly lower plasma density compared to their surrounding areas. Because of the inhomogeneous distribution of ionospheric plasma, EPBs can disrupt satellite communication and Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) signals, affecting both the phase and amplitude of the signals. This interference results in increased errors of positioning accuracy for GNSS receivers, and rendering satellite communications and navigation services unusable in severe cases. This phenomenon is commonly referred to as ionospheric scintillation. Therefore, monitoring EPBs is essential to ensuring the stability of satellite communications and the reliability of positioning, navigation, and communication services.  
However, accurately detecting the locations of these EPBs remains a challenging task.  To address these challenges, Center Weather Administration (CWA) employs data from both space-based and ground-based sources to validate for the locations of plasma bubbles, including scintillation geolocation data from FORMOSAT-7/COSMIC-2 measurements, as well as ionospheric scintillation data from GNSS ground-based receivers. Additionally, full-band chromatic all-sky imager built in Lulin observatory (23.5°N, 120.9°E, 12.5°N magnetic latitude), which detects OI 630.0 nm airglow emissions, is used for verification. By combining these types of observations, it becomes possible to track the movement of EPBs and expand the monitoring regions surrounding Taiwan. This method is likely to be a valuable tool in providing information for local space weather services.
